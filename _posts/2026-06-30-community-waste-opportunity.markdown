# What If Your Community's Waste Was Energy?

Every day, your community throws away resources that could power homes and fertilize fields. It's not a future scenario — it's happening right now in cities across the country. The question is whether we're ready to reimagine what we do with the food scraps, yard trimmings, and organic waste that fill our bins.

Most of us don't think much about where our trash goes. We put it in a bin, it gets collected, and it disappears. But here's what actually happens: when food and yard waste end up in landfills without oxygen, bacteria break it down and produce methane — a greenhouse gas roughly 28 times more potent than CO₂ over a century. That methane rises into the atmosphere. Meanwhile, all the nutrients and potential energy locked in that waste are lost forever.

## The Scale of the Opportunity

One-third of the food produced globally never gets eaten — roughly 2.5 billion tonnes per year. If food waste were a country, it would rank third in global greenhouse gas emissions, behind only China and the United States. In the average American household of four people, about 30-40% of the food purchased becomes waste, costing roughly $1,500 per year in uneaten meals.

Add yard waste to the picture, and the opportunity becomes enormous. Grass clippings, leaves, tree trimmings, garden debris — municipalities collect tens of millions of tons annually. When landfilled, this organic matter follows the same trajectory as food waste: anaerobic decomposition, methane release, lost resources.

But what if your community approached waste differently?

## The Path Forward Exists Today

Unlike many climate challenges, this one doesn't require waiting for new technology. The solutions are available now — composting programs, anaerobic digestion systems, and municipal waste diversion initiatives already proven in cities and regions worldwide. These aren't experimental concepts. They're working systems that turn waste into two things communities desperately need: renewable energy and nutrient-rich fertilizer.

Composting programs redirect organic waste from landfills and produce soil amendment that improves water retention and supports local agriculture. Anaerobic digestion goes further — it captures biogas that can fuel vehicles, heat buildings, or generate electricity while also producing a digestate byproduct with even more concentrated nutrients than compost.

The barriers aren't technological. They're logistical and cultural. Cities and communities need reliable collection infrastructure, processing capacity, and markets for the end products. They need clarity about what materials can be accepted. And they need residents and businesses to participate.

## Why This Matters for Your Community

Water scarcity is becoming a defining challenge for cities worldwide. Urban populations facing water shortages are projected to grow from 933 million people in 2016 to over 1.6 billion by 2050. When nutrient-rich digestate is applied to land — whether in agricultural fields, urban green spaces, or landscaping projects — it improves soil structure and water retention, reducing the need for freshwater irrigation. In a world of increasing water stress, that's not a minor benefit. That's essential resilience.

Similarly, as municipalities grapple with waste management costs and landfill capacity, diverting organic materials to composting or digestion reduces disposal expenses while generating revenue through energy sales or compost/digestate distribution.

## What Communities Are Learning

The successful examples share a common pattern: they start with honest assessment of their waste streams, invest in collection and processing infrastructure, and build partnerships across the supply chain. Some focus primarily on composting. Others add anaerobic digestion to capture energy alongside nutrient recovery. The specifics vary, but the principle is consistent — organic waste is too valuable to bury.

Your community generates food waste and yard waste every single day. The question isn't whether that waste could become a resource. The question is whether you're ready to treat it as one.

This isn't about guilt or sacrifice. It's about recognizing that the organic materials in our trash are inputs for solutions we already need: renewable energy, soil health, and climate action. The infrastructure and expertise exist. The science is clear. What's required now is the collective decision to shift from disposal to recovery.

The conversation is changing. Cities and organizations are beginning to recognize that preventing waste — and recovering value from what we do generate — is one of the highest-leverage climate actions available. If your community isn't part of that conversation yet, now is the time to start.
