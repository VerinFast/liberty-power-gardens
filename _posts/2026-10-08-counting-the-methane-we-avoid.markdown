Most environmental progress is easy to photograph: a new solar array, a restored wetland, a bike lane. Diverting food waste isn't. The main benefit is something that *doesn't* happen. Methane never forms in a landfill. Gas never leaks through a cap. The warming never adds up. That makes it one of the most effective climate actions a community can take, and one of the hardest to explain at a budget hearing. The good news is that the tools for counting what never happens have gotten much better, and communities can use them.

## A Quarter of the Waste, More Than Half the Problem

Start with the number that changes how people think about landfills. According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, food makes up only about a quarter of what goes into municipal solid waste landfills. Yet it accounts for an estimated 58 percent of the methane those landfills release into the atmosphere. The reason is speed. Food breaks down quickly, so much of its methane is produced early, often before gas collection systems are installed on that section of the landfill. EPA's research also found something telling: total methane emissions from landfills have been declining, but the share coming from landfilled food waste has been *rising*. Food waste is the stubborn part of the landfill methane problem, and diverting it is the most targeted way to address it.

## From Tons Diverted to Emissions Avoided

For years, most diversion programs reported their success in tons: how many tons of food scraps were collected, composted, or digested. Tons matter, but they don't say much about climate impact on their own. EPA now publishes an Avoided Landfilled Food Waste Methane Emissions Calculator, a free spreadsheet tool built on the same landfill and decay assumptions as its national research. It's designed so that local governments, schools, institutions, and businesses can estimate the methane reductions tied to each ton of food waste kept out of a landfill. EPA's broader Waste Reduction Model (WARM) does similar accounting across many materials and management pathways. Using these tools, a community can turn "we diverted 2,000 tons this year" into an emissions figure that can be compared against the rest of its climate plan.

## Why the Timeline Matters in the Math

These tools also capture something tonnage counts miss: landfill methane doesn't appear all at once. Organic material buried today keeps emitting for decades, so the benefit of diverting it builds over time. A ton kept out of the landfill this year avoids a stream of emissions that would otherwise have continued for years. And because methane is far more potent than carbon dioxide in the short term, avoiding it early has an outsized effect on near-term warming. Good accounting reflects that. It treats diversion as a lasting reduction, not a one-year credit, which is closer to how the atmosphere actually experiences it.

## Measurement Makes Programs Durable

Measurement matters beyond the climate report. Programs that can show their impact in terms officials and residents care about tend to survive budget cycles. Programs that can't tend to get cut. An avoided-emissions figure, placed next to the avoided tipping fees and hauling costs a community already tracks, makes a stronger case than either number alone. It also helps a community compare its options honestly: preventing waste in the first place, rescuing edible food, composting, and digestion each have a place. Clear accounting helps decide where each ton does the most good. For material that can't be prevented or rescued, sending it through anaerobic digestion adds two more benefits to the ledger: energy recovered from the gas, and digestate that returns nutrients and water to the soil.

## Making the Invisible Visible

The challenge with avoided emissions has always been that they're invisible. They're the gas that never formed and the warming that never happened. But invisible isn't the same as unmeasurable. With credible, peer-reviewed tools now freely available, communities don't have to rely on hoping their diversion efforts are working. They can count the results, report them, and build on them year after year. The methane we keep from being produced won't show up in a photo, but it can show up in the numbers, and that's where lasting commitments get made.
