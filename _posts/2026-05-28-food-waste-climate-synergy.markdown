## One Number That Changes Everything

Food systems account for 60% of human-caused methane emissions. That's larger than the entire energy sector.

The methane doesn't come from sitting down to a meal—it comes from what never makes it to the table. Between 34% and 40% of all food produced globally goes uneaten. In landfills, that uneaten food becomes a methane factory. A single apple rotting in oxygen-free conditions produces methane; multiply that across billions of tons, and you're staring at a climate problem with a surprisingly simple solution.

More than 150 countries, representing 70% of the global economy, have committed to cutting methane emissions by 30% by 2030. But here's the critical insight: this isn't just a climate pledge. It's a shift in how communities recognize and recover value from organic material.

## The Business Case Emerges

New Jersey became the latest state to mandate food waste diversion—companies and institutions now legally cannot landfill organics. California has diverted over 2 million tons in the past five years. Massachusetts' composting infrastructure expansion is generating jobs and carbon benefits simultaneously.

What's driving the momentum isn't just climate guilt—it's economics. Food waste has 3 to 15 times more biogas energy potential per ton than other organic feedstocks like cattle manure or biosolids. From an anaerobic digestion perspective, food waste is premium feedstock.

The U.S. has over 310 AD facilities operating today, up from 154 just seven years ago. Nearly all are processing food waste. A single facility processing 100 tons of food waste per day can generate enough energy to power 800 to 1,400 homes annually. Scale that across a region, and you're looking at genuine energy infrastructure.

## The Community Angle

The transformation isn't happening in giant centralized plants. Food waste diversion infrastructure is hyperlocal. Retail chains, restaurants, grocery distributors, and municipal programs are creating collection points in neighborhoods. Waste reduction becomes visible; the connection between local action and global climate impact becomes tangible.

When a restaurant diverts its daily food scraps to a nearby digester instead of the landfill, it's not abstract climate policy—it's a concrete decision to convert waste into energy that serves the same community.

This is where individual choice and systemic change align. Participate in your community's food waste program. Support local restaurants and retailers that divert organics. Ask your municipality about AD infrastructure. The climate benefit is undeniable. The energy security upside is real. And the infrastructure is already being built by communities just like yours.
