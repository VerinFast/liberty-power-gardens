Every municipal waste budget has two numbers hiding inside it. One is the tipping fee, the price a landfill charges at the gate for every ton that crosses its scale. The other is the haul: the trucks, drivers, diesel, and hours it takes to get that ton there in the first place. Most of the conversation about waste costs focuses on the first number. But for food waste in particular, the two work together in a way that quietly punishes communities for doing nothing, and it's worth pulling them apart to see why.

## The Gate Price Keeps Climbing

Tipping fees are not a fixed cost. According to the Environmental Research & Education Foundation, the national average landfill tipping fee rose about 10% in 2024 to roughly $62 per ton, the largest single-year jump in several years. The Northeast pays the most, at more than $80 per ton on average, and individual counties have announced increases well beyond that as local capacity tightens. When a nearby landfill fills up or closes, the next one is farther away and often more expensive. That means communities pay more per ton and drive farther to deliver each one.

## You're Paying to Haul Water

This is where food waste becomes a uniquely bad deal. Food scraps are heavy because they are mostly water, commonly around 70% or more by weight. A landfill charges by the ton, so a community is paying tipping fees on that water. A collection truck also hits its weight limit sooner with wet organics than with dry trash, so the same volume of material takes more trips, more fuel, and more labor to move. Every mile added to the route multiplies that cost. The result is a line item that grows from both ends: a higher price at the gate and a longer, heavier trip to get there.

## Shrinking the Ton and the Trip

The most direct way to cut both costs is to stop sending organics on the long haul at all. A containerized anaerobic digester like the LPG Core is designed to sit close to where food waste is generated, whether that's a neighborhood, a campus, a market, or a municipal yard. Material that would have gone into a landfill truck is processed locally instead. Every ton diverted is a ton that never pays a tipping fee. Keeping heavy, wet organics off long-distance routes cuts the trips, the fuel, and the wear that come with them. What leaves the digester is also more useful than what went in: biogas that can be used as energy, and liquid digestate that returns nutrients and water to local soil instead of shipping that water to a landfill.

## A Budget Line That Pays Back

There's a climate benefit riding along with the savings. Food buried in a landfill keeps producing methane for decades. Digesting it under controlled conditions means that future methane is never produced in an open landfill, and the energy in it is put to use. Fewer truck miles also mean fewer tailpipe emissions. For once, the fiscally cautious choice and the climate choice point in the same direction.

## The Cheapest Ton Is the One You Don't Ship

Communities can't control what landfills charge or how far away the next open cell will be. They can control how much material they put on the truck. Every ton of food waste handled locally is a ton that escapes both the gate price and the mileage. As disposal costs keep rising, the question for municipal budgets is shifting from how cheaply organics can be thrown away to why they're being shipped anywhere at all.
